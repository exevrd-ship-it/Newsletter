Why I started this newsletter

Every week, the news is full of economic headlines: a war pushes up oil prices, a central bank raises rates, unemployment ticks up, a government announces a new budget. Most of us read the headline and move on, because the explanation is either missing or buried in jargon.

I wanted to understand what was really going on, so I started writing it down for myself. Then for a few friends. Then for anyone curious enough to open an email on a Sunday morning.

The goal is simple: after six minutes, you should understand the week a little better than everyone who only read the headlines.

Each issue mixes a Weekly Recap of the main economic stories with either an Econ 101 explainer on a timeless topic or, once a month, a Monthly Finance review of the markets. It's written for readers aged roughly 16 to 25: students, young professionals, and anyone who wants to finally get what "quantitative tightening" means.

Experience

Writing about the economy is one thing; seeing how it works up close is another. A few experiences that shaped how I think about money:

  1. Intern Independent wealth management firm · Lyon

    Observed how advisers build savings and investment strategies for families: risk profiles, diversification, life insurance and retirement planning. My first real lesson in how interest rates and markets affect ordinary households.

  2. Intern Caisse d'Épargne · Retail banking

    Shadowed advisers in a branch: savings accounts, consumer and mortgage loans, client meetings. A front-row view of how ECB decisions end up in the rates people are actually offered.

  3. Student job Part-time, alongside school

    Working with customers taught me reliability, time management, and what a price increase looks like from the other side of the counter.

Editorial principles

An economics newsletter is only useful if you can trust it. These are the rules I hold myself to:

Neutral, always

I explain the arguments; I don't tell you who to vote for. Where economists disagree, I say so.

Sourced

Figures come from primary sources: INSEE, Eurostat, the ECB, the IMF, the OECD. Every chart lists its source.

Clear before clever

If a term needs a definition, it gets one. No jargon for the sake of sounding smart.

Corrections

If I get something wrong, I correct it openly in the next issue. Spot an error? Please write to me.
