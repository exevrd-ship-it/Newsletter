Chart of the Week · Issue #24

Ten years of euro-area money, in one picture.

From negative interest rates to the fastest tightening in the ECB's history: how inflation and the price of money moved together between 2015 and 2024.

What this chart tells us

  1. A decade of "too little" inflationFrom 2015 to 2020, inflation stayed well below the ECB's 2% target. To encourage lending and spending, the central bank pushed its deposit rate below zero.
  2. 2022: the shockReopening after Covid, supply bottlenecks and the energy crisis linked to the war in Ukraine sent inflation to 8.4%, the highest annual rate since the euro was created.
  3. The responseThe ECB raised its rate from -0.5% to 4% in just over a year. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, which cools demand and, eventually, prices.
  4. The lagNotice the gap: rates rose after inflation had already jumped. Monetary policy works with a delay of many months, which is why central banks try to look ahead.

Why it matters for you

The deposit rate is the interest the ECB pays banks on money parked overnight. It sounds technical, but it sets the tone for nearly every other rate in the economy: the interest on a Livret A, the cost of a student loan, the mortgage rate on a first flat.

When the rate was negative, saving earned almost nothing and borrowing was cheap. When it climbed to 4%, savers were finally rewarded, but buying a home became much more expensive. Same economy, very different decisions.

How to read it

The navy line is the problem (prices rising). The gold line is the medicine (the price of money). When the navy line climbs far above 2%, expect the gold line to follow, with a delay.
