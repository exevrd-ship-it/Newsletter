Imagine a baguette costs €1.00 this year and €1.05 next year. Nothing about the baguette has changed: same flour, same size, same bakery. What changed is the value of the euro in your pocket. That, in one sentence, is inflation: **a general rise in prices, which means each euro buys a little less than before**.

:::box The short version
- Inflation measures how fast prices rise on average, usually over a year.
- A little inflation (around 2%) is considered healthy; too much, or prices falling, causes problems.
- Central banks fight high inflation mainly by raising interest rates.
:::

## How is it measured?

Statisticians don't track just one baguette. In France, INSEE follows the prices of a huge "basket" of goods and services that a typical household buys: food, rent, energy, transport, clothes, phone plans, haircuts. Each item is weighted by how much of their budget people spend on it. In the euro area, Eurostat publishes a harmonised version called the **HICP** so countries can be compared fairly.

When you read "inflation was 2.4% last year", it means this basket cost, on average, 2.4% more than twelve months earlier. Your personal inflation can be quite different: if you don't drive, a jump in fuel prices affects you less.

## Why do prices rise?

Economists usually describe two big families of causes:

- **Demand-pull:** people and businesses want to buy more than the economy can produce. Too much money chasing too few goods, so sellers can raise prices.
- **Cost-push:** producing things becomes more expensive (energy, raw materials, wages), and companies pass some of those costs on to customers. The 2022 energy shock in Europe was a textbook example.

Expectations matter too. If everyone expects prices to rise 5% next year, workers ask for bigger raises and firms raise prices in advance, which can make inflation stick.

## Why not aim for zero?

It sounds tempting: why not keep prices perfectly stable? The problem is the opposite risk, **deflation**. When prices fall, people delay purchases ("it'll be cheaper next month"), firms sell less, cut jobs, and the economy can spiral downwards. Debts also become heavier in real terms.

> A small, predictable amount of inflation acts like oil in the engine: it keeps things moving without overheating.

That's why the European Central Bank targets **2% inflation over the medium term**. It's low enough that people barely notice it day to day, and high enough to keep a safety margin above zero.

## How do central banks fight it?

The main tool is the interest rate. When the ECB raises its rates, borrowing becomes more expensive for banks, which then charge more for mortgages and business loans. People and companies borrow and spend less, demand cools, and price rises slow down. When inflation is too low, the ECB does the reverse.

It's a slow and imperfect tool. Rate changes take many months to work through the economy, so central bankers have to act on forecasts, a bit like steering a large ship. You can see this delay clearly in our [Chart of the Week](chart-of-the-week.html).

## So what does it mean for you?

Inflation quietly affects almost every financial decision:

- **Savings:** if your account pays 1.5% while inflation runs at 3%, your money is actually losing purchasing power.
- **Wages:** a 2% raise during a year of 5% inflation is, in real terms, a pay cut.
- **Borrowing:** inflation erodes the real value of debt, but the higher interest rates that come with it make new loans more expensive.

Next time you see "inflation" in a headline, try asking two questions: *is it driven by demand or by costs?* and *what will the central bank do about it?* You'll already be reading the news like an economist.

:::box Word of the week
**Purchasing power**: the amount of goods and services a sum of money can buy. Inflation reduces it; rising wages can restore it.
:::
